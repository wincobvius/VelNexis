You used ChatGPT for an assignment, a cover letter or an office email — and now the worry: can people tell?

## The honest answer

Sometimes, but not reliably, and not the way you might fear. AI detectors are inaccurate in both directions: they flag fully human writing as AI, and they miss AI-assisted writing, regularly. No detector score proves anything — and most institutions increasingly know this.

What actually exposes AI-assisted work in practice is the mismatch: a style suddenly different from your usual writing, claims without your usual examples, or details you cannot discuss when asked.

## The safer way to use AI

1. Use it for structure, research directions and rough drafts — then write the final text yourself, in your own words, with your own examples.
2. Add what AI cannot supply: your local context, your data, your references, your voice.
3. Follow your institution's or employer's stated policy. Where the rules say "disclose AI use," disclosing is safer than any disguise trick — policies, not detectors, are what actually judge you.

## Worth knowing

Presenting AI output as fully original work can violate academic or workplace rules even when no detector catches it. The risk is a rules problem more than a technology problem.

## Quick recap
- Detectors exist but are wrong often enough that serious institutions treat them as hints, not proof.
- The real rule is your school or workplace policy - ask before using AI, not after.
- Editing AI output heavily and adding your own facts reduces both risk and dishonesty.
- The safe standard: use AI to explain and plan, write the final text yourself.
