A message lands — from a "friend," a client, an online match — and something feels off. Too smooth, too general, oddly formal. Is a real person writing, or a script?

## The honest answer

There is no reliable way to be sure — not for you, and not for the "AI detector" tools, which regularly flag human writing as AI and miss AI writing entirely. What you can do is weigh signals, and verify identity when it matters.

## Signals that raise suspicion

- Perfectly uniform grammar, with none of the messiness of real typing
- General talk that could apply to anyone — no personal references, no local context
- Overly formal connectors and polished phrasing in what should be a casual chat
- The "friend" dodging any concrete detail about shared history

None of these prove anything alone. Scammers can type badly; real people can write beautifully.

## When it matters, verify properly

1. Ask something only the real person would know — a shared memory, an inside reference.
2. Move the conversation to a channel you already trust: call the number you already have for them.
3. Never send money, codes or documents because of chat pressure alone. That rule catches more scams than any detector ever will.

## Quick recap
- Common signs: perfectly even sentences, zero personal details, and phrases like 'in today's fast-paced world'.
- AI detectors are unreliable and regularly accuse real writers - never treat them as proof.
- For important messages, verify the facts inside, not just the style of the writing.
- The same tools that write it can humanize it - so style alone can never be the verdict.

The strongest signal is missing specificity - no names, no dates, no personal detail that could only come from the sender. Style can be faked; real personal context cannot.
