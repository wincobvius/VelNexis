The phone rings. It sounds exactly like your son, your brother, your mother — panicked, mid-emergency, asking you to send money right now. It may really be them. It may also be a voice-clone scam: AI can copy a person's voice from a short clip posted online, and scammers have used exactly this to pressure families.

## What to do in the moment

1. Hang up — or at least commit to not sending money while the call is live. Urgency is the scammer's main weapon; real emergencies survive a two-minute delay.
2. Call that family member yourself on their regular number. In most reported cases the real person picks up, confused and safe.
3. Cannot reach them? Check with another family member or their workplace before moving any money.
4. Agree on a family code word now — before any emergency — that a caller in crisis must say before you act on a money request.

## If money was already sent

Contact your bank immediately, then report to your national cybercrime service (in Pakistan: FIA cybercrime wing, 1991). Speed helps, but no one can promise recovery.

## The honest limitation

A familiar voice no longer proves identity — and a strange call is not automatically fake either. The voice cannot be the proof; only an independent callback can.

## Quick recap
- Agree on a family code word now - one word ends any fake emergency call instantly.
- Hang up and call back on the real number - the clone cannot answer the real line.
- Scammers create urgency and secrecy - both are red flags, always.
- Never move or send money based on a voice call alone, even a familiar voice.
