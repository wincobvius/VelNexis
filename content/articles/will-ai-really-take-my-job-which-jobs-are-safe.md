Every week brings a new headline: AI writes like humans, AI passes exams, AI replaces teams. The worry — "will a machine replace me?" — deserves a better answer than panic or false comfort.

## Separate what we know from what we guess

Observable now: AI is very good at narrow, well-specified digital tasks — drafting text, summarizing, basic code, routine data work. Genuinely uncertain: how fast that expands, and what new roles appear around it. Past technology shifts — spreadsheets, ATMs, the web — changed work dramatically while employment adapted overall. History is a guide, not a guarantee.

Notice how numbers mutate as they travel: a claim like "40% of work tasks could be automated" sounds like "40% of people will lose their jobs" by the time it reaches a headline. Those are completely different statements.

## Which work is more exposed today

- More exposed: repetitive, fully digital tasks — basic data entry, templated writing, routine analysis.
- Less exposed so far: work needing physical presence, human trust, or judgment under responsibility — trades, healthcare, teaching, skilled repair.

## The plan that works either way

Learn the AI tools in your own field. Whatever the future holds, the person who can use these tools well holds an edge over the person who cannot — true today, regardless of how the bigger story unfolds.

## Quick recap
- Jobs built on routine information work face the most pressure; hands-on and judgment-heavy work faces less.
- History pattern: automation removes tasks, then creates new roles nobody predicted.
- The practical move: learn the AI tools of your own field this year.
- Jobs that fix, care, negotiate and decide in messy real-world situations stay human longest.
